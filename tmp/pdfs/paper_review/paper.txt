Abstract—Autonomous vehicles rely on multiple sensors, such
as cameras, LiDAR, radars, and GPS, to perceive their surround-
ings and navigate safely. Combining information from diverse
sensors enhances situational awareness by mitigating individual
sensor limitations. This research focuses on improving the ac-
curacy of object detection, classification, and tracking by fusing
LiDAR and camera data using deep learning techniques.This
paper investigates the fusion of LiDAR and camera data us-
ing attention bottlenecks. By combining complementary sensor
strengths, the approach addresses individual limitations and noisy
data challenges. The method, validated on the KITTI dataset,
demonstrates improved perception accuracy and robustness.
Key contributions include implementation of a scalable fusion
framework, advancing reliable decision-making for autonomous
navigation in complex scenarios.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Autonomous vehicles are typically equipped with various
sensors that aid in safe navigation by perceiving the envi-
ronment. These sensors include cameras, LiDAR, and radar,
among others. To ensure a fail-safe system, multiple identical
sensors may be installed. Cameras capture color information
during the day, while night vision cameras use IR rays to pro-
vide grayscale images at night. LiDAR scans the environment
and generates a 3D point cloud representation, while radar
provides range and angle information.
However, each sensor has limitations in its usage. For
example, camera systems provide good images during the day
but degrade in low light, rainy conditions, or at night. LiDAR
generates accurate 3D information, but processing point clouds
for analysis is computationally costly compared to camera
systems. Therefore, using individual sensors has advantages
and disadvantages in capturing accurate environmental details.
To address this, many attempts have been made to fuse
information from multiple sensors and improve various types
of analysis operations on the sensor data. The primary focus
of our work is on improving the accuracy of analysis activities
such as object detection, classification, and tracking. By fusing
data from multiple sensors, the system can obtain a more
comprehensive view of the environment and improve the accu-
racy of these analysis activities. In summary, this experimental
work aims to investigate the use of sensor fusion to improve
the accuracy of analysis activities in autonomous vehicles.
By combining data from multiple sensors, the system can
generate more accurate and reliable data, which can improve
the effectiveness and applicability of autonomous vehicles in
complex environments.
II. M ULTI-SENSOR DATA FUSION
Multi-sensor data fusion(MSDF) [1] is a technique that
integrates data from several sensors to achieve a more accurate
and comprehensive understanding of a specific environment or
scenario. The aim of multi-sensor data fusion is to enhance
the system’s overall performance by utilizing complementary
information from different sensors, which leads to better
decision-making and improved situational awareness. This
technology finds its applications in several domains, including
autonomous vehicles, defense, robotics, healthcare, among
others.
Multi-sensor data fusion has gained significant attention in
recent years, due to its ability to address complex real-world
problems. With the rapid advancement of sensor technology,
there are now many types of sensors available, such as
cameras, LiDARs, radars, microphones, and more, which can
provide diverse and complementary information about the en-
vironment. By integrating information from multiple sensors,
multi-sensor data fusion can compensate for the limitations
and uncertainties of individual sensors, reduce ambiguity, and
enhance the overall accuracy and reliability of the system.

V. L ITERATURE SURVEY
Many papers have proposed various fusion techniques to
improve the accuracy and robustness of object detection and
tracking. For example, in the paper ”Multi-sensor Fusion for
Object Detection and Tracking in Autonomous Driving,” [5]
the authors propose a deep learning-based approach that fuses
LiDAR and camera data for object detection and tracking.
A recent trend in the literature is the use of deep learning-
based methods for multi-sensor data fusion. For example,
in the paper ”Deep Sensor Fusion for Autonomous Driving:
A Survey,” [3] the authors provide a comprehensive survey
of recent deep learning-based sensor fusion methods for au-
tonomous driving and their performance. The paper discusses
different fusion techniques, such as Kalman filtering, particle
filtering, and deep learning methods. Some works propose
to use the camera and LiDAR data together for semantic
segmentation, which means classifying each pixel of an image
into different object classes. For example, in the paper ”Real-
time Semantic Segmentation for Autonomous Driving using
LiDAR and Camera” [6], the authors propose a system that
fuses the LiDAR and camera data and uses it for semantic
segmentation in real-time. Some works propose to use the
camera and LiDAR data together for semantic segmentation,
which means classifying each pixel of an image into different
object classes. For example, in the paper ”Real-time Semantic
Segmentation for Autonomous Driving using LiDAR and
Camera” [6], the authors propose a system that fuses the
LiDAR and camera data and uses it for semantic segmentation
in real-time. In their paper [7] ”Attention Bottlenecks for
multimodal fusion,” authors address the problem of fusing data
of different modalities using bottleneck latents to collate and
condense relevant information and share what is necessary.
VI. M ETHODOLOGY OVERVIEW
A transformer based approach [7] is used in this work to
combine LiDAR and camera data for classification tasks in
autonomous vehicles. The model takes as camera image frame
and LiDAR point cloud as input. Point cloud is converted
to pseudo image for reducing computational demands. Image
frame and pseudo image (derived from pointcloud) are divided
into patches and tokenised to facilitate computation. A set of
fusion tokens (Bottleneck tokens) [7] are inserted with LiDAR
and camera tokens. With the help of attention bottlenecks in
transformer, the model is forced to carry out cross modal atten-
tion through fusion tokens. Final layer’s output is forwarded
to a classifier.
A. Model Architecture
As mentioned earlier, we used a fusion transformer [7]
to combine LiDAR and camera data for classification tasks.
Architecture of fusion transformer is shown in Figure 9. The
model takes as input streams of images and point clouds,
where the continuous input streams (i.e., image frames and
point clouds) are divided into patches to facilitate computation.
This approach of dividing images into patches and using
transformers for image classification was demonstrated in the
Vision Transformer (ViT) model [8]. A similar approach is
applied in processing both point clouds and image streams.
Point clouds consist of a series of unordered 3D coordinates.
Due to the computationally intensive nature of processing
point clouds, it is converted histogram based pseudo images.
Pseudo-histogram images (derived from point clouds) and the
camera image frames are provided as inputs to the fusion
model. These images are resized and then tokenized before
being processed. Pretrained Vision Transformer (ViT) [8] is
used in this work for feature extraction and tokenization.
In addition to LiDAR and camera tokens (xlidar, ximage) , a
small set of fusion tokens are added in the input sequence.
Fusion tokens(Bottleneck tokens) xfusion are introduced to
restrict cross modal attention flow happens through them using
attention bottlenecks [7] in the fusion transformer. These atten-
tion bottlenecks facilitate cross-modal attention flow, enabling
the model to focus on relevant regions of the multi modal data.
The bottlenecks play a key role by emphasizing important
features from both modalities. Cross-modal attention [9] is
effective in identifying critical regions where both camera
and LiDAR features interact, helping the model to extract
complementary information from each modality. The cross-
modal attention [9] blocks guide the model in sharing critical
information between the two modalities, ensuring that the most
relevant features are communicated and fused effectively.
Representation at output layer is finally passed to classifier
for classification tasks.

C. Input Representations
LiDAR pointcloud is an unstructured set of points, where
each point is represented by spatial coordinates(x,y,z). These
coordinates describe the position of objects in the environment
relative to the sensor’s origin. Optionally, the point cloud
may include additional attributes such as intensity, which
represents the reflectivity of the object’s surface. Due to the
computationally intensive nature of processing point clouds,
various methods have been proposed to reduce this complexity.
For example, the paper [10] proposes a method to convert
point clouds into histogram images for easier processing. This
transformation allows the data to be compatible with stan-
dard 2D convolutional operations, making it easier to extract
meaningful features. While models like PointNet [11], Point-
Pillers [12] directly process point clouds without transforming
them into another format. We followed approach leverages
the conversion to pseudo-histogram images to simplify the
computational demands of point cloud processing.
The 3D data is projected onto a 2D grid, where the x
and y-axes define the spatial extent of the scene, and the
z-coordinate is used to segment points into distinct height
layers (e.g., ”above” and ”below” thresholds). This grid-
based representation serves as the foundation for constructing
histograms,where each cell represent point density or counts
within each grid cell. Histogram image is 3D tensor with shape
(3,448,224), where channels represents below, above, and total
features in the cell.
Image data is in RGB format is resized to tensor with shape
(3x224x224).
D. Output Representations
Transformer-based model processes the bottleneck-
enhanced embeddings, capturing relationships within and
across modalities. The fused embeddings are passed to task-
specific heads, such as classification layers for downstream
tasks.
VII. D ATASET
We experiment model using KITTI dataset [4], a widely
used benchmark in autonomous driving research. It is created
by the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology and the Toyota
Technological Institute at Chicago. It includes a collection of
images and LIDAR data, which are utilized in computer vision
research for tasks such as stereo vision, optical flow, visual
odometry, 3D object detection, and 3D tracking. Datasets are
captured by driving through the mid-sized city of Karlsruhe,
as well as in rural areas and on highways. Data collected from
two color and two grayscale cameras (10 Hz, resolution: 1392
× 512 pixels, field of view: 90° × 35°), a Velodyne HDL-
64E 3D laser scanner (10 Hz, 64 beams, range: 100 m), a
GPS/IMU localization unit. In addition to providing the data,
benchmarks, evaluation metrics for each task are also offered.

VIII. I MPLEMENTATION DETAILS
The model is implemented in PyTorch and tested using the
KITTI dataset [4]. It uses separate backbone networks for each
modality. ResNet-18 pretrained on ImageNet is used on image
frames, while PointPillars is used on point clouds. The image
frame is resized to a maximum size of 1280 × 384 while
maintaining its aspect ratio. These backbones encode each
modality into feature embeddings. A camera frame and its
associated point cloud are sampled together from the dataset.
A 16 × 16 token grid is formed from the point-cloud pseudo-
image, while the image features are geometrically aligned
with the corresponding bird’s-eye-view cells using the KITTI
camera-to-LiDAR calibration and four height queries. In the
pseudo-image, the x-axis spans (0, 69.12) meters and the y-
axis spans (−39.68, 39.68) meters. Each cell is (0.16, 0.16)
meters in the x and y directions.
The pseudo-image encoder takes the four-dimensional point
features and processes them using a PillarFeatureNet. The
encoded pillar features are scattered into a bird’s-eye-view
pseudo-image and processed by a SECOND backbone and
SECONDFPN neck. The resulting high-level LiDAR feature
representation contains 384 channels and preserves the spatial
structure required for 3D object detection.
The attention bottlenecks are implemented by a class called
SymmetricMBTBEVFusion. The SymmetricMBTBEVFusion
class uses four learnable bottleneck tokens and PyTorch’s
nn.MultiheadAttention to fuse point-cloud and RGB
features. Both modality feature maps are projected to an
embedding dimension of 128 and represented by geometrically
aligned 16 × 16 token grids. Each modality concatenates its
feature tokens with the shared bottleneck tokens and processes
them using separate transformer parameters. The bottleneck
updates produced by the point-cloud and RGB branches are
averaged after each fusion layer. Four fusion layers with eight
attention heads are used. The fused representation is then
used to update the LiDAR tokens through an additional cross-
attention step. Camera information is communicated to the
LiDAR representation through the shared bottleneck tokens. A
lightweight 1 × 1 residual projection maps the fused features
back to 384 channels and adds them to the original LiDAR
BEV feature map. The bottleneck tokens are initialized with
a standard deviation of 0.02, and the residual projection is
initialized to zero. During training, 15% of the LiDAR tokens
in camera-visible regions and 10% of the LiDAR BEV feature
channels are randomly dropped. An auxiliary class-specific
camera-BEV center-prediction loss with a weight of 0.25 is
also applied. This design ensures efficient, parameter-effective
feature fusion while preserving the PointPillars detection rep-
resentation.
The model was initialized using the best LiDAR-only Point-
Pillars checkpoint. The LiDAR encoder and detection head
were frozen for the first five epochs and subsequently fine-
tuned with the complete model. The base learning rate was set
to 0.0003, with a learning rate of 0.0006 for the camera and
fusion components and 0.00015 for the LiDAR and detection
components. The model was trained for 40 epochs. The model
was trained with a physical batch size of 6 and eight-step
gradient accumulation, resulting in an effective batch size of
48.
IX. R ESULTS AND ANALYSIS
A. Quantitative Results
The LiDAR-only PointPillars reference and the attention-
bottleneck fusion detector were evaluated on the 3,769-sample
KITTI validation split using AP40. Both models used the same
validation split, object classes, point-cloud range, effective
batch size, random seed, and KITTI evaluator. The best
checkpoint for each model was selected using overall 3D AP40
at moderate difficulty. The LiDAR-only reference reached its
best result at epoch 28, while the fusion detector reached its
best result at epoch 20. The overall results are presented in
Table I.
As shown in Table I, the attention-bottleneck fusion detector
outperforms the LiDAR-only reference at every BEV and 3D
difficulty level. At moderate difficulty, it improves BEV AP 40
by 1.7604 points and 3D AP 40 by 3.9828 points. The latter is
a 10.30% relative improvement over the LiDAR-only result.
The per-class moderate 3D AP 40 results are presented in
Table II.
As shown in Table II, fusion improves moderate 3D AP 40
for all three classes. The largest absolute improvement is
observed for Car, followed by Cyclist and Pedestrian. The best
fusion checkpoint obtains a moderate 3D AP 40 of 42.6696 at
epoch 20, compared with 41.5993 at epoch 40. This decrease
indicates mild overfitting during the second half of training
and supports validation-based checkpoint selection.
B. Camera Ablation
The best fusion checkpoint was reevaluated with correct
camera images, all-black images, and a deterministic seed-0
derangement of the validation images. In the shuffled condi-
tion, every LiDAR sample receives an image from a different
validation sample while its LiDAR points and annotations
remain unchanged. The results of these experiments are pre-
sented in Table III.
As shown in Table III, shuffling the camera input reduces
moderate BEV AP 40 by 0.6672 points and moderate 3D AP 40
by 0.5742 points. It changes the predictions in 3,716 of the
3,769 validation frames, demonstrating that image information
influences the detector. The strongest class-specific effect is
observed for Pedestrian, whose moderate 3D AP 40 decreases
from 32.2419 to 30.4090 under image shuffling. The corre-
sponding changes for Car and Cyclist are small and are not
consistently positive. Thus, useful camera information is most
evident for pedestrian detection, while LiDAR remains the
dominant modality overall.

D. Discussion and Limitations
The results in Tables I and II show that the proposed
attention-bottleneck fusion detector performs better than the
LiDAR-only reference on the KITTI validation split. Camera
ablations demonstrate a measurable contribution from visual
content, particularly for pedestrians. However, the ablation gap
is smaller than the total improvement over LiDAR-only, so the
full gain cannot be attributed to camera information alone. The
additional attention layers, bottleneck representation, LiDAR-
side processing, warm start, and staged fine-tuning may also
contribute.
The experiments use one random seed and the validation
split rather than the official KITTI test server. Consequently,
variation across training runs has not been measured. Addi-
tional seeds and comparisons with alternative fusion architec-
tures are required before claiming that attention bottlenecks are
generally superior to other fusion methods. Attention weights
should also be interpreted as model diagnostics rather than
causal explanations.
X. F UTURE WORK
This work explored the fusion of LiDAR and camera data
using attention bottlenecks. Future work will include more
extensive ablation studies to evaluate the effects of the num-
ber of bottleneck tokens, attention heads, fusion layers, and
fusion-stage placement. The model should also be evaluated
across multiple random seeds and compared with alternative
fusion architectures to better establish the effectiveness of the
proposed approach.
Another direction is to incorporate temporal sensor data
[13]. Processing consecutive camera frames and LiDAR scans
could allow the model to use motion information, potentially
improving the detection and tracking of moving or partially
occluded objects.
Finally, future experiments could compare fusion at different
stages of the network. Earlier fusion may preserve more
low-level cross-modal information, whereas later fusion may

XI. C ONCLUSION
We implemented and evaluated a transformer-based fusion
technique for LiDAR and camera data fusion for autonomous
vehicles. We experimented with cross-modal attention [14]
using attention bottlenecks. The fusion model outperformed
the LiDAR-only baseline on the KITTI validation split. There
is still scope for future work to improve the performance of
the fusion model and develop better strategies for fusing data
from multiple modalities.
